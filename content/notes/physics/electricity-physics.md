## Static Electricity

![Illustration of static electricity](/note-images/physics/static-electricity.png "Placeholder illustration: static electricity")

**Static electricity** is the build-up of electric charge on the surface of an object. It occurs when electrons are transferred by friction between two insulating materials. When two materials are rubbed together, electrons move from one to the other. One material gains electrons and becomes negatively charged. The other loses electrons and becomes positively charged.

Objects with the same charge repel each other. Objects with opposite charges attract each other. This is because charged objects create an **electric field**. An electric field is the region around a charged object where it exerts a force on other charged objects.

## Current Electricity

![Illustration of an electric circuit](/note-images/physics/circuits.png "Placeholder illustration: current electricity")

**Electric current** is the flow of electric charge. It is measured in amperes (A) using an ammeter. Current flows in a complete circuit. In a circuit, current is the same at all points in a series circuit.

**Potential difference**, also known as voltage, is the electrical push that drives the current around a circuit. It is measured in volts (V) using a voltmeter. A cell or battery provides potential difference.

**Resistance** is the opposition to the flow of current. It is measured in ohms (Ω). A higher resistance means less current flows for a given potential difference. For a metal wire at constant temperature, the relationship is:

$$V = I R$$

## Series and Parallel Circuits

![Illustration of electric circuits](/note-images/physics/circuits.png "Placeholder illustration: series and parallel circuits")

In a **series circuit**, the components are connected in a single loop. The current is the same through all components. The potential difference is shared between the components. If one component breaks, the circuit is broken and all components stop working.

In a **parallel circuit**, the components are connected in separate branches. The current splits across the branches. The potential difference is the same across each branch. If one branch breaks, the other branches continue to work. Most household circuits are parallel circuits.

## Magnetism and Electromagnets

![Illustration of an electromagnet](/note-images/physics/electromagnets.png "Placeholder illustration: magnetism and electromagnets")

**Magnets** have two poles, called the north pole and the south pole. Like poles repel each other. Opposite poles attract each other. The magnetic field is the region around a magnet where it exerts a force on other magnets or magnetic materials. Magnetic field lines go from the north pole to the south pole outside the magnet.

An **electromagnet** is a coil of wire carrying a current, usually wrapped around an iron core. The iron core becomes magnetised. The strength of an electromagnet can be increased in three ways. Increasing the number of coils increases the strength. Increasing the current increases the strength. Using an iron core instead of a non-magnetic core increases the strength.

Electromagnets are useful because they can be switched on and off. They are used in electric bells, cranes, and relays.
