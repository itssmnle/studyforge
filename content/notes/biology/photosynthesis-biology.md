## Photosynthesis

![Illustration of photosynthesis in a leaf](/note-images/biology/photosynthesis.png "Placeholder illustration: photosynthesis")

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants and algae make their own food. They use energy from sunlight to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen.

The word equation for photosynthesis is:

$$\text{Carbon dioxide} + \text{Water} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \text{Glucose} + \text{Oxygen}$$

The balanced chemical symbol equation is:

$$\ce{6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2}$$

Photosynthesis takes place inside **chloroplasts**, which contain a green pigment called **chlorophyll**. Chlorophyll absorbs sunlight and transfers its energy into chemical energy stored in glucose.

The glucose produced is used in several ways. It can be used immediately for respiration to release energy. It can be converted into starch for storage. It can be used to make cellulose for cell walls. It can also be combined with other substances to make proteins and lipids for growth.

### Adaptations of Leaves

Leaves are the main organs of photosynthesis. They have several adaptations that make them highly efficient at this process.

Leaves have a **large surface area** to absorb as much sunlight as possible. They are **thin**, so gases can diffuse quickly and easily. They contain many **chloroplasts**, especially in the upper layers, to trap sunlight. The **waxy cuticle** on the surface reduces water loss. **Stomata** are tiny pores on the lower surface that allow carbon dioxide to enter and oxygen to leave. **Veins** transport water and minerals to the leaf and carry away the glucose produced.

## Interdependence in Ecosystems

![Illustration of an ecosystem food web](/note-images/biology/ecosystems.png "Placeholder illustration: interdependence in ecosystems")

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms interacting with each other and their non-living environment. All organisms in an ecosystem are interconnected and depend on each other for survival. This is called interdependence.

### Food Chains and Food Webs

A **food chain** shows the direct flow of energy from one organism to another. It always starts with a producer. **Producers** are organisms that make their own food, usually through photosynthesis. Green plants are producers. **Consumers** are organisms that eat other organisms. A **primary consumer** eats a producer. A **secondary consumer** eats a primary consumer. A **tertiary consumer** eats a secondary consumer.

An example of a food chain is:

> Grass → Rabbit → Fox

Grass is the producer. The rabbit is the primary consumer. The fox is the secondary consumer.

A **food web** is made up of multiple interconnected food chains. It shows the complex feeding relationships in an ecosystem more accurately than a single food chain.

Energy is transferred from one organism to the next along a food chain. However, not all energy is passed on. Some energy is lost at each stage. It is used for respiration, growth, movement, or is lost as heat. This is why food chains rarely have more than four or five stages. It also explains why there are usually fewer organisms at higher stages of the food chain.

### Food Security and Pollination

Plants are essential for human food security, which means having reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food. Many of the crops we eat, such as fruits, vegetables, and nuts, rely on insect pollination to produce seeds and fruits.

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen from the male part of a flower to the female part. When insects such as bees, butterflies, and hoverflies visit flowers to collect nectar, they carry pollen from one flower to another. This fertilises the flower and allows it to produce seeds and fruits.

Without insect pollinators, many of our crop plants would fail to produce food. This would lead to food shortages and higher prices. Protecting pollinator populations is therefore vital for global food security. The decline in pollinator numbers due to habitat loss, pesticide use, and disease is a major concern.

## Environmental Impact

![Illustration of an ecosystem](/note-images/biology/ecosystems.png "Placeholder illustration: environmental impact")

Organisms both affect and are affected by their environment. Changes in the environment can have significant consequences for the organisms that live there.

### Bioaccumulation

**Bioaccumulation** is the gradual build-up of toxic substances in an organism. This happens when an organism absorbs a toxic substance more quickly than it can break it down or excrete it. The substance accumulates in the organism's tissues.

Bioaccumulation is particularly dangerous in food chains because of a process called **biomagnification**. This is when the concentration of a toxic substance increases as it passes from one trophic level to the next. The top predators in a food chain, such as large fish, birds of prey, and humans, end up with the highest concentrations of toxins.

A classic example is the pesticide DDT. DDT was used to kill insects. It does not break down easily in the environment. Small amounts were absorbed by plants and small organisms. These were eaten by small fish, which were eaten by larger fish, which were eaten by birds. The DDT built up in the birds' bodies. It caused their eggshells to become thin and fragile, leading to a dramatic decline in bird populations.

### Other Environmental Impacts

Organisms also affect their environment in other ways. Plants can change the composition of the soil by adding nutrients from their fallen leaves. Animals can alter their environment through burrowing, which aerates the soil. Human activities have some of the most significant impacts on the environment. These include deforestation, pollution, and the release of greenhouse gases that contribute to climate change.
