## Heredity and DNA

![Illustration of DNA and inheritance](/note-images/biology/dna-and-inheritance.png "Placeholder illustration: heredity and DNA")

Heredity is the passing of genetic information from parents to offspring. This is why offspring resemble their parents.

Genetic information is stored in a molecule called **DNA**, which stands for deoxyribonucleic acid. DNA has a double helix structure, which looks like a twisted ladder. It is found in the nucleus of every cell.

DNA is organised into structures called **chromosomes**. Humans have 46 chromosomes in each body cell, arranged in 23 pairs. One chromosome from each pair is inherited from the mother and the other from the father.

**Genes** are sections of DNA. Each gene carries the code for a specific characteristic, such as eye colour or blood type. Genes are the basic units of heredity.

### The Discovery of DNA Structure

The structure of DNA was discovered in 1953. Several scientists made vital contributions.

**Rosalind Franklin** produced an X-ray diffraction image, known as Photo 51, which revealed the helical shape of DNA. Her work was crucial but not fully recognised at the time.

**Maurice Wilkins** worked with Franklin and shared her data with other scientists. He helped confirm the double helix structure.

**James Watson** and **Francis Crick** used Franklin's data and Wilkins's insights to build a model of DNA. They proposed the double helix structure that we know today. They were awarded the Nobel Prize for their work, along with Wilkins.

## Variation

![Illustration of variation within a species](/note-images/biology/variation.png "Placeholder illustration: variation")

Variation refers to the differences between individuals of the same species. These differences can be caused by genetic factors, environmental factors, or a combination of both.

### Continuous Variation

**Continuous variation** is when a characteristic has a range of values between two extremes. There are no distinct categories. For example, height is a continuous characteristic. Individuals can be any height within a range. Weight and shoe size are also examples of continuous variation. These characteristics are usually influenced by many genes and also by environmental factors like diet.

### Discontinuous Variation

**Discontinuous variation** is when a characteristic falls into distinct categories with no intermediates. For example, blood group is discontinuous. A person can have blood group A, B, AB, or O. There is no value in between. Other examples include eye colour in many species and the ability to roll your tongue. These characteristics are usually controlled by a single gene and are less affected by the environment.

## Natural Selection and Evolution

![Illustration of evolution](/note-images/biology/evolution-and-biodiversity.png "Placeholder illustration: natural selection and evolution")

**Evolution** is the process by which species change over time. It is driven by **natural selection**.

Natural selection is often described as survival of the fittest. It works like this. Individuals within a species show variation. Some individuals have characteristics that are better suited to their environment. These individuals are more likely to survive and reproduce. They pass their advantageous characteristics on to their offspring. Over many generations, these beneficial characteristics become more common in the population. The species gradually changes, or evolves.

For natural selection to occur, there must be variation within a species. There must also be competition for resources, such as food, water, and space. Individuals with advantageous characteristics are better able to compete and therefore have a higher chance of survival and reproduction.

Over millions of years, natural selection has led to the enormous diversity of life on Earth. It explains how organisms become adapted to their environments.

## Biodiversity and Extinction

![Illustration of biodiversity](/note-images/biology/evolution-and-biodiversity.png "Placeholder illustration: biodiversity and extinction")

**Biodiversity** is the variety of life on Earth. It includes the number of different species and the genetic variation within species. A high level of biodiversity is essential for healthy ecosystems. It provides stability, resources, and services that humans depend on, such as food, medicine, and clean air and water.

### Extinction

**Extinction** occurs when a species dies out completely. This means there are no living individuals left anywhere on Earth.

Extinction can happen for many reasons. **Environmental changes** can make a species's habitat unsuitable. For example, climate change, deforestation, or a volcanic eruption can destroy habitats. A species may become extinct if it cannot adapt to the new conditions quickly enough. **Competition** with other species can also cause extinction. If a new species is introduced that is better adapted to the environment, it can outcompete the native species for resources. **Disease** can also wipe out a species, especially if the population is small and has low genetic diversity.

### Maintaining Biodiversity

Maintaining biodiversity is crucial for the health of the planet. There are several ways to protect biodiversity. These include protecting habitats from destruction and pollution. Establishing national parks and nature reserves helps to preserve natural ecosystems. Laws and international agreements can limit the hunting of endangered species. Reducing pollution and tackling climate change are also essential for protecting biodiversity.

### Gene Banks

Gene banks are facilities that store genetic material. They are used to preserve hereditary materials for the future. Gene banks can store seeds, sperm, eggs, or DNA samples. They are important for conserving biodiversity. If a species becomes extinct in the wild, its genetic material may still be preserved in a gene bank. This material could potentially be used to reintroduce the species in the future. Gene banks also preserve the genetic diversity of crop plants. This is valuable for breeding new varieties that are resistant to disease or adapted to changing climates.
