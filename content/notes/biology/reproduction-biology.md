## Human Reproduction

![Illustration of human reproduction](/note-images/biology/human-reproduction.png "Placeholder illustration: human reproduction")

Human reproduction involves the joining of two specialised sex cells, called gametes. The male gamete is the **sperm cell**. The female gamete is the **egg cell**, or ovum.

### Male Reproductive System

The male reproductive system produces and delivers sperm. The **testes** produce sperm and the hormone testosterone. Sperm are stored in the **epididymis** before travelling through the **sperm duct**. Glands add fluids to the sperm to create semen. The **penis** delivers semen into the female reproductive system.

### Female Reproductive System

The female reproductive system produces eggs and supports the developing foetus. The **ovaries** release one egg cell approximately every 28 days. The **oviducts**, or Fallopian tubes, are where fertilisation usually takes place. The egg travels down the oviduct to the **uterus**, or womb. The uterus has a muscular wall and a soft lining where the fertilised egg implants and grows. The **cervix** is the opening at the bottom of the uterus. The **vagina** receives sperm during sexual intercourse and is also the birth canal.

### Gametes

**Sperm cells** are adapted for their function. They have a head containing the genetic material and a tail for swimming. They also contain many mitochondria to provide energy for swimming.

**Egg cells** are much larger than sperm cells. They contain a large food store to nourish the developing embryo. When a sperm cell fuses with an egg cell, fertilisation occurs. The fertilised egg is called a **zygote**. It contains a full set of chromosomes, half from each parent.

### Fertilisation, Gestation, and Birth

**Fertilisation** happens when a sperm cell penetrates and fuses with an egg cell. This usually takes place in the oviduct. The resulting zygote begins to divide and travel down towards the uterus. It implants into the thick, soft lining of the uterus.

The developing organism is now called an **embryo**, and later a **foetus**. **Gestation** is the period of development inside the uterus. In humans, this lasts approximately 40 weeks. The foetus receives oxygen and nutrients from the mother through the **placenta**. The placenta is an organ that develops in the uterus during pregnancy. It allows substances to pass between the mother's and foetus's blood without the blood mixing directly. This exchange happens through the **umbilical cord**.

**Birth** begins when the muscles of the uterus wall contract. These contractions push the baby out through the cervix and vagina. After the baby is born, the placenta and umbilical cord are also expelled.

### Maternal Lifestyle and the Foetus

The placenta allows substances to pass from the mother's blood to the foetus's blood. This means that anything the mother consumes can affect the foetus.

**Alcohol** can cross the placenta and damage the foetus. Excessive alcohol consumption can lead to Foetal Alcohol Syndrome, which causes developmental problems and facial abnormalities.

**Smoking** is harmful to the foetus. Nicotine and carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke reduce the amount of oxygen reaching the foetus. This can lead to restricted growth, premature birth, and low birth weight.

A **poor diet** can also affect the foetus. The foetus needs a good supply of vitamins and minerals for healthy development. For example, folic acid is important in early pregnancy to prevent neural tube defects.

## The Menstrual Cycle

![Illustration of the menstrual cycle](/note-images/biology/menstrual-cycle.png "Placeholder illustration: the menstrual cycle")

The menstrual cycle is a roughly 28-day cycle that prepares the female body for pregnancy. It is controlled by hormones.

The cycle begins on day 1, the start of **menstruation**, or the period. The lining of the uterus is shed and leaves the body through the vagina.

Around day 5, the lining of the uterus begins to thicken again. An egg begins to mature in one of the ovaries.

Around day 14, **ovulation** occurs. This is when the mature egg is released from the ovary. The egg travels down the oviduct towards the uterus. This is the time when fertilisation is most likely to occur.

If fertilisation does not happen, the egg dies and the lining of the uterus breaks down. The cycle then repeats. The uterus lining is thickest around the time of ovulation. This is to provide a nourishing environment for a fertilised egg to implant.

## Plant Reproduction

![Illustration of pollination in a flower](/note-images/biology/plant-reproduction.png "Placeholder illustration: plant reproduction")

Plants reproduce sexually through flowers. Flowers contain the male and female reproductive organs.

### Flower Structure

The **male parts** of a flower are called the **stamens**. Each stamen consists of a **filament**, which is a thin stalk, and an **anther** at the top. The anther produces pollen grains, which contain the male gametes.

The **female parts** of a flower are called the **carpels**. Each carpel consists of the **stigma**, **style**, and **ovary**. The stigma is the sticky top part that catches pollen. The style is the tube that connects the stigma to the ovary. The ovary contains ovules, which contain the female gametes.

Other important parts of a flower include the **petals**, which attract insects, and the **sepals**, which protect the flower bud before it opens.

### Pollination

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma. This can happen in two ways.

**Insect pollination** occurs when insects, such as bees, visit flowers to collect nectar. Pollen grains stick to their bodies and are carried to the stigma of another flower. Insect-pollinated flowers are usually brightly coloured and scented. They often have large, sticky pollen grains.

**Wind pollination** occurs when pollen is carried by the wind. Wind-pollinated flowers are usually small, dull, and produce large amounts of light, smooth pollen. They often have feathery stigmas to catch pollen from the air. Grasses and many trees are wind-pollinated.

### Fertilisation and Seed Formation

After pollination, a pollen grain on the stigma grows a **pollen tube** down the style to the ovary. The male gamete travels down this tube and fuses with the female gamete in the ovule. This is **fertilisation**.

After fertilisation, the ovule develops into a **seed**. The seed contains the embryo plant and a food store. The ovary wall develops into a **fruit**, which protects the seed.

### Seed Dispersal

Seeds must be dispersed, or spread out, to reduce competition for water, light, and nutrients with the parent plant.

**Wind dispersal** occurs when seeds are light and have adaptations like wings or parachutes. Examples include dandelion seeds and sycamore seeds.

**Animal dispersal** occurs when seeds are eaten and pass through the digestive system, or when they have hooks that attach to animal fur. Examples include berries and burdock seeds.

**Water dispersal** occurs when seeds are able to float. Coconuts are a classic example.

**Explosive dispersal** occurs when a seed pod dries out and bursts open, scattering the seeds. Peas and poppies use this method.
