## States of Matter

![Illustration of particle arrangement](/note-images/chemistry/particle-arrangement.png "Placeholder illustration: states of matter")

All matter is made up of tiny particles. The way these particles are arranged and how they move determines whether a substance is a solid, a liquid, or a gas.

In a **solid**, the particles are packed closely together in a regular arrangement. They vibrate in fixed positions but do not move around. This gives solids a fixed shape and volume.

In a **liquid**, the particles are still close together but are arranged randomly. They can move and slide past each other. This allows liquids to flow and take the shape of their container, but they have a fixed volume.

In a **gas**, the particles are far apart and arranged randomly. They move rapidly and freely in all directions. This means gases have no fixed shape or volume. They expand to fill any container.

## Changes of State

![Illustration of particles changing state](/note-images/chemistry/changes-of-state.png "Placeholder illustration: changes of state")

When a substance is heated or cooled, it can change from one state to another. These are physical changes, meaning no new substances are formed.

**Melting** is the change from a solid to a liquid. This happens when the particles gain enough energy to break free from their fixed positions.

**Freezing** is the change from a liquid to a solid. Particles lose energy and become fixed in place.

**Boiling** and **evaporation** are changes from a liquid to a gas. Boiling happens throughout the liquid at a specific temperature. Evaporation happens only at the surface and can occur at any temperature.

**Condensing** is the change from a gas to a liquid. Particles lose energy and come closer together.

**Subliming** is the change from a solid directly to a gas, skipping the liquid state. Dry ice and iodine are examples.

Heating and cooling curves show how the temperature of a substance changes over time as it is heated or cooled. Flat sections on the curve indicate a change of state, where energy is being used to break or form bonds between particles rather than raising the temperature.

## Gas Pressure and Diffusion

![Illustration of particles diffusing](/note-images/chemistry/diffusion-and-density.png "Placeholder illustration: gas pressure and diffusion")

Gas particles are constantly moving and colliding with each other and with the walls of their container. These collisions create **gas pressure**. Increasing the temperature of a gas gives particles more energy, making them move faster and collide more forcefully. This increases the pressure. If the container is made smaller, the particles collide with the walls more frequently, also increasing the pressure.

**Diffusion** is the movement of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration. It happens in gases and liquids. Particles spread out until they are evenly distributed. Diffusion explains why smells travel across a room and why ink spreads through water.
