## Chemical vs Physical Changes

![Illustration of reactants and products](/note-images/chemistry/reactants-and-products.png "Placeholder illustration: chemical and physical changes")

A **physical change** is a change that does not produce a new substance. Melting, freezing, and dissolving are physical changes. They are usually reversible.

A **chemical reaction** produces one or more new substances. The new substances have different properties from the reactants. Chemical reactions are often irreversible. Signs of a chemical reaction include a colour change, a temperature change, bubbles of gas, and the formation of a precipitate.

## Representing Reactions

![Illustration of chemical formulae](/note-images/chemistry/chemical-formulae.png "Placeholder illustration: representing reactions")

Chemical reactions can be represented using word equations and symbol equations. The substances at the start are called **reactants**. The substances at the end are called **products**. An arrow is used to show the direction of the reaction.

$$\text{Reactants} \rightarrow \text{Products}$$

For example, the word equation for the reaction between hydrogen and oxygen is:

$$\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$$

A balanced symbol equation shows the same number of each type of atom on both sides of the equation.

## Reaction Types

![Illustration of reaction types](/note-images/chemistry/reaction-types.png "Placeholder illustration: reaction types")

**Combustion** is a reaction in which a substance burns in oxygen. It releases energy as heat and light. The products are usually carbon dioxide and water.

**Thermal decomposition** is a reaction in which a compound is broken down into simpler substances by heating. For example, heating calcium carbonate produces calcium oxide and carbon dioxide.

**Oxidation** is a reaction in which a substance gains oxygen. Rusting is an example of oxidation, where iron reacts with oxygen to form iron oxide.

**Displacement** is a reaction in which a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive element in a compound. For example, when iron is added to copper sulfate solution, iron displaces copper.

## Acids, Alkalis, and Neutralisation

![Illustration of a chemical reaction](/note-images/chemistry/reaction-types.png "Placeholder illustration: acids, alkalis, and neutralisation")

The **pH scale** measures how acidic or alkaline a substance is. The scale goes from 0 to 14. Acids have a pH below 7. Alkalis have a pH above 7. Neutral substances have a pH of exactly 7.

**Neutralisation** is the reaction between an acid and an alkali. It produces a salt and water.

$$\text{Acid} + \text{Alkali} \rightarrow \text{Salt} + \text{Water}$$

Acids react with metals to produce hydrogen gas. The **squeaky pop test** is used to identify hydrogen. When a burning splint is held near the gas, it makes a squeaky pop sound.

Acids react with carbonates to produce carbon dioxide gas. The **limewater test** is used to identify carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide turns limewater cloudy.

## Catalysts

![Illustration of a chemical reaction](/note-images/chemistry/reaction-types.png "Placeholder illustration: catalysts")

A **catalyst** is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up itself. The catalyst is not changed by the reaction. It can be used again and again. Catalysts lower the activation energy needed for a reaction to start. Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up reactions in living organisms.
