## 1. Writing Large Numbers in Figures

### What does this mean?
Writing a number in **figures** means writing it using digits ($0$–$9$) instead of words. A digit's **place value** depends on its position within the number. Numbers are grouped into sets of three digits from right to left: **millions**, **thousands**, and **units**.

![Diagram: Place value chart showing Millions (Hundred thousands, Ten thousands, Thousands) and Units (Hundreds, Tens, Units) with an example number 5,321,565 split across columns](/maths-images/Y7/07-writing-large-numbers-in-figures.png)

---

### Method
1. Break the spoken/written number into its period groups (**millions**, **thousands**, **units**).
2. Convert each group into digits.
3. Ensure every group after the first contains exactly **three digits** (add leading zeros if needed).
4. Combine the groups separated by commas.

---

### Worked Example

**Question:**
Write *five million, eighty-two thousand and nineteen* in figures.

**Answer:**
* **Millions group:** $5$
* **Thousands group:** $082$ *(add leading zero for 3 digits)*
* **Units group:** $019$ *(add leading zero for 3 digits)*

Combined result: **$5,082,019$**

> [!INFO] **Examiner Tips and Tricks**
> Every group after the first millions group must have exactly $3$ digits! A common mistake is writing $5,82,19$ instead of $5,082,019$.

## 2. Writing Large Numbers in Words

### What does this mean?
Writing a number in **words** means translating digits into spoken English using place value names (**million**, **thousand**).

![Diagram: Writing large numbers in words](/maths-images/Y7/06-writing-large-numbers-in-words.png)

---

### Method
1. Split the number into groups of three digits from right to left using commas.
2. Read each $3$-digit group as a normal number followed by its group name (**million**, **thousand**).
3. Connect the final units group using the word **"and"**.

---

### Worked Example

**Question:**
Write $6,403,021$ in words.

**Answer:**
* $6 \rightarrow$ **six million**
* $403 \rightarrow$ **four hundred and three thousand**
* $021 \rightarrow$ **twenty-one**

Combined result: **Six million, four hundred and three thousand and twenty-one**

## 3. Rounding to Powers of 10

### What does this mean?
Rounding a number to a power of $10$ ($10$, $100$, $1000$, etc.) means finding the closest multiple of that power of $10$.

![Diagram: Rounding to powers of ten](/maths-images/Y7/05-rounding-powers-of-ten.png)

---

### Method
1. Identify the **target column** you are rounding to.
2. Look at the **decider digit** directly to its right:
   * If the decider digit is **$5$ or more**, round **up** (add $1$ to the target digit).
   * If the decider digit is **less than $5$**, round **down** (keep the target digit the same).
3. Change all digits to the right of the target column to **zeros**.

---

### Worked Example

**Question:**
Round $47,382$ to the nearest thousand.

**Answer:**
* Target digit (thousands): $7$
* Decider digit (hundreds): $3$
* Since $3 < 5$, round down (keep $7$ as $7$).
* Replace all digits after $7$ with zeros.

Answer: **$47,000$**

## 4. Comparing Decimals and Inequality Symbols

### What does this mean?
Comparing numbers involves determining which value is larger or smaller using mathematical inequality symbols:
* $=$ (equal to)
* $\neq$ (not equal to)
* $<$ (less than)
* $>$ (greater than)
* $\le$ (less than or equal to)
* $\ge$ (greater than or equal to)

![Diagram: Vertical comparison of 4.307 and 4.370 with decimal points aligned and an arrow highlighting the hundredths column where 0 is less than 7](/maths-images/Y7/09-comparing-decimals.png)

---

### Method
1. Line up the numbers vertically by their **decimal points**.
2. Add **trailing zeros** to shorter decimals so all numbers have the same number of decimal places.
3. Compare digits column-by-column from **left to right** until you find a difference.

---

### Worked Example

**Question:**
Insert the correct symbol ($<$ or $>$) between $4.307$ and $4.37$.

**Answer:**
1. Add trailing zero: $4.37 \rightarrow 4.370$.
2. Compare columns from left to right:
   * Units ($4$) and Tenths ($3$) are equal.
   * Hundredths: $0$ is less than $7$ ($0 < 7$).
3. Therefore, $4.307 < 4.370$.

Answer: **$4.307 < 4.37$**

> [!INFO] **Examiner Tips and Tricks**
> Adding trailing zeros to shorter decimals makes comparing them much easier! E.g., comparing $4.307$ and $4.370$ is clearer than comparing $4.307$ and $4.37$.

## 5. Standard Form

### What does this mean?
**Standard form** is a concise way of writing very large or very small numbers using powers of $10$. It is always written as:

$$a \times 10^n$$

* $a$ must be between $1$ and $10$ ($1 \le a < 10$).
* $n$ must be an integer (whole number).

![Diagram: Standard form layout breaking down 'a' (must be between 1 and 10) and 'n' (integer power showing decimal places moved)](/maths-images/Y7/08-standard-form.png)

---

### Method

#### Converting Large Numbers ($n > 0$):
1. Move the decimal point **left** until you get a number $a$ where $1 \le a < 10$.
2. Count the number of places moved; this gives your positive power $n$.

#### Converting Small Numbers ($n < 0$):
1. Move the decimal point **right** until you get a number $a$ where $1 \le a < 10$.
2. Count the number of places moved; this gives your negative power $-n$.

---

### Worked Example

**Question 1:** Convert $6,200,000$ into standard form.  
**Answer:** Move decimal point $6$ places left $\rightarrow$ **$6.2 \times 10^6$**

**Question 2:** Convert $0.000082$ into standard form.  
**Answer:** Move decimal point $5$ places right $\rightarrow$ **$8.2 \times 10^{-5}$**

> [!INFO] **Examiner Tips and Tricks**
> A negative power of $10$ represents a small decimal starting with $0.$, not a negative number. The negative index $-n$ equals the total number of zeros at the start of the number, including the zero before the decimal point! E.g., $10^{-5}$ has $5$ zeros: **$0.0000$**$82$.
